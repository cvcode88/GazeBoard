How Gaze Board works

A quick tour of every feature. The mouse stands in for eye-gaze throughout - hovering is "looking," clicking is a stand-in for a deliberate blink.

Looking vs. selecting

Typing mode (top bar): hover a key long enough and its ring fills in, then it's typed - that hold time is the dwell time. Blink to select swaps this out entirely: hovering never selects anything by itself, only a click does, so you can look freely with zero risk of typing something by accident. The two are mutually exclusive - turning one on turns the other off.

The keyboard

Common letters sit centered and larger, rare ones near the edges and smaller, based on real English letter frequency (switch to the alphabetical layout in Settings to compare). Gold highlighting on a key means the prediction algorithm thinks it's likely to come next - more solid, more confident.

Getting words out faster

Suggested words (left panel by default) complete the word you're typing. Favorites (right panel) hold whole phrases you've starred for one-click reuse - tap the star next to the output box to save whatever you've typed. Greyed-out ghost text right after your cursor previews the rest of your word or the likely next word - click it to accept. All three - suggestions, favorites, ghost text - always require a click, regardless of typing/blink mode, since gaze rests on them constantly while you compose.

The dock

space, back, clear and speak sit below the keyboard - speak reads your sentence aloud with the browser's built-in voice. Auto-spacebar (in Settings) can press space for you once a word can't extend into a longer one.

Calibration

Run it from the top bar or Settings. It times how quickly you reach five spread-out targets, then has you spell three short words, and uses both to set a starting dwell time tuned to your pace. From there, the adaptive dwell AI keeps nudging it based on your ongoing backspace rate and typing speed.

Settings

Tabbed by purpose: Appearance (color scheme, accent color, contrast, colorblind-friendly confidence color, panel placement, keyboard size), Input (layout, dwell timing, auto-spacebar), Predict (next-letter highlighting), Session (calibration and reset controls), and Stats (session and all-time typing metrics for your profile).

Accounts

Everything - settings, calibration, favorites, and dwell-timing history - is saved per signed-in profile, in this browser only. There's no list of other people's names to browse; you sign in or create an account by typing the exact username.

Simulated webcam replaces the small dot with a fuzzy, wandering blob approximating what a plain camera (no IR illumination or corneal-reflection tracking) actually outputs, instead of the mouse's perfect coordinates - and it isn't just cosmetic: the blob's own jittered position, not the real cursor, is what the keyboard actually hit-tests for dwell selection, so a wrong guess here is a wrong letter there. Within the blob, color runs darkest and most solid at the center (the actual estimate) and fades toward its edge (the surrounding uncertainty) - like a real confidence heatmap. Its hue shifts blue as recent movement speed increases (lower confidence mid-saccade) and orange as the mouse settles into a steadier fixation. Shape isn't fixed either: it's a little taller than wide even at rest (video-based trackers measure vertical gaze less precisely than horizontal - smaller range of motion, harder to detect), and stretches further along whatever direction it's currently moving, more so the faster it moves (webcam frame rate and processing lag mean the reported position blurs and trails behind the real one during fast eye movement) - with a softly morphing outline so it reads as a blob settling rather than a shape shaking in place. Real webcam replaces all of that with an actual eye tracker: WebGazer.js (Brown University, open source since 2016) reads your real webcam through your browser and predicts where you're looking, using the exact same blob and key-selection pipeline described above - it's genuinely driving the keyboard, not a demo. Video never leaves this device; everything runs locally in your browser. It needs camera permission and a moment to load its face-tracking model, gets more accurate the more you use it, and its accuracy depends entirely on your real webcam, lighting and camera angle, not the slider below.
Both ends are pulled from published webcam-eye-tracking studies, not guesses: the low end (~340px error) reflects sparse-calibration/poor-condition results reported for WebGazer.js-style tracking; the high end (~100px, roughly 2° of visual angle) reflects the best documented accuracy for calibrated, well-lit webcam-only tracking in the same body of research. For comparison, a dedicated low-cost IR tracker like the Gazepoint GP3 HD measures around 1° (roughly 35-40px) - meaningfully tighter than the best a plain webcam gets, because it's fundamentally different hardware, not just a nicer camera.

The small video box in the corner is WebGazer's own view of your face - it's how you can tell it's genuinely tracking you rather than faking it. Every letter, dock button, suggestion and favorite is gaze-driven while this is on: sustained gaze selects a key (typing mode) or a click confirms whatever you're looking at (blink mode) - the click can come from anywhere, a real switch included, since gaze is doing the pointing. If the camera prompt never appears, or nothing ever tracks, this page is probably embedded in a viewer that blocks camera access - try opening it in its own browser tab.
